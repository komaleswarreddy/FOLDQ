# FoldQ — results

> **For peptide folding at today's hardware scale, how do variational quantum algorithms
> actually compare against classical and quantum-inspired classical solvers on the
> identical problem instance?**

All numbers below are generated by `foldq report` directly from the committed
`benchmark-full.json` artifact: 6 sequences × 2 encodings × 3 solvers × 50 seeds.

## Verdict

**Ballistic simulated bifurcation — the quantum-inspired solver, and the one most
directly relevant to quantum-inspired silicon — loses.** Across the whole sweep it
reaches the exact optimum on exactly one instance class: six-bead chains under the
one-hot encoding, at p_s = 0.90. Everywhere else it scores **0.00**, and from N = 7
upward the best energy it finds is *positive* — an infeasible conformation, not merely a
poor fold. At N = 10 its best is +703 against an optimum of −2.

Simulated annealing given a structure-aware move set solves the same instances at
**p_s = 1.00 up to N = 9**.

The one place bSB works is itself the second finding. At N = 6 it scores **0.00 on the
dense encoding and 0.90 on the one-hot encoding** — the same instance, the same physics,
the same ground state, differing only in how a turn is written into qubits.

## Why

The dense encoding writes a turn in two qubits, which is optimal, but that makes the turn
indicators quadratic, the pairwise distance quartic and the gated interaction term
quintic. A QUBO or Ising model is quadratic by definition, so the Hamiltonian must be
degree-reduced, and Rosenberg reduction pays for that in auxiliary variables carrying
penalty weights ~10⁴ above the physical energy scale. A hydrophobic contact is worth −1.
On that landscape it is a rounding error, and the solver optimises the penalties instead.

The one-hot encoding makes every indicator a single qubit, so the Hamiltonian is 3-local
rather than 5-local and needs far less reduction. It costs twice the turn qubits and
still ends up with **fewer variables in total** — 117 against 156 at N = 10 — because it
needs so many fewer auxiliaries.

## The result that cuts both ways

Ranking the encodings depends on the solver's move set, and the ranking *reverses*:

| N | encoding | naive annealing | slaved annealing | bSB |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 6 | dense | 0.86 | **1.00** | 0.00 |
| 6 | one-hot | 0.72 | **1.00** | **0.90** |
| 8 | dense | 0.02 | **1.00** | 0.00 |
| 8 | one-hot | 0.04 | 0.82 | 0.00 |
| 9 | dense | 0.00 | **1.00** | 0.00 |
| 9 | one-hot | 0.02 | 0.58 | 0.00 |
| 10 | dense | 0.00 | 0.14 | 0.00 |
| 10 | one-hot | 0.00 | 0.14 | 0.00 |

On the raw QUBO, one-hot is the better encoding — it is the only one bSB can solve at
all. Under a move set that slaves the auxiliary variables to the primaries, dense is the
better encoding, holding 1.00 where one-hot has fallen to 0.58. Slaving makes the
effective search space the primary register, and dense has 14 of those against one-hot's
24. **The encoding that is friendlier to a naive solver is the harder one for a solver
that exploits the structure.**

Slaving is legitimate rather than a trick: auxiliaries are *determined* by the primaries,
and repairing them to their defining products drives every Rosenberg penalty to zero, so
it provably cannot worsen the objective.

## What this says about quantum-inspired hardware

The brief's closing question is why a company would build quantum-inspired classical
silicon rather than a quantum computer. This benchmark answers a sharper version of it.

An Ising machine — simulated bifurcation in silicon, a coherent Ising machine, an
annealer — is fast because it does one thing: evolve a *quadratic* model with reasonably
homogeneous couplings. Any problem that is not natively quadratic has to be forced into
that shape, and the forcing is where the cost lands. Here it cost a 10⁴ dynamic range in
the couplings, exactly the regime such machines are not built for. The measured
consequence is bSB scoring 0.00 on eleven of the twelve instance-encoding combinations.

General-purpose hardware is not obliged to accept the quadratic form, and that is its
advantage. It can slave variables, exploit structure, and change its move set per
problem. Fixed-function Ising hardware cannot.

So the honest case for quantum-inspired silicon is narrower than the marketing: strong
when the problem is natively quadratic and homogeneous, and it evaporates when the
encoding forces a penalty hierarchy. **Choosing the encoding is the engineering work;
choosing the solver is comparatively easy.**

## Scope: what is not here

This build covers M0–M3 and M6 of the brief: the lattice model, a validated Hamiltonian
in three equivalent representations, the classical and quantum-inspired solvers, and the
benchmark harness.

**There is no variational quantum solver.** No VQE, no QAOA, no CVaR, no noise model, no
error mitigation. The headline question above is therefore answered only for its
classical half. Shipping a half-built VQE is precisely what the brief warns against, and
the comparison against a quantum device remains future work.

## Validation

These results are only meaningful because the model underneath them is checked against
things this repository did not choose:

- **Lattice geometry and self-avoidance** reproduce published self-avoiding walk counts
  exactly — cubic against [OEIS A001412](https://oeis.org/A001412) through nine steps
  (1, 6, 30, 150, 726, 3534, 16926, 81390, 387966, 1853886), diamond against counts
  reconstructed from [A227715](https://oeis.org/A227715) and
  [A227716](https://oeis.org/A227716).
- **The Hamiltonian's distance metric** agrees with the independently implemented lattice
  geometry over 104 372 bead pairs, reproducing Eq. SI-14 of the reference paper.
- **Symmetry fixing** reduces the search by exactly 12.000× at every chain length, the
  order of the tetrahedral rotation group A₄, with the optimum unchanged.
- **Every solver's answer** is scored against exhaustive enumeration, which is exact at
  these sizes.
- **Penalty weights are derived**, and a test sets one below its derived bound and shows
  the ground state falls below the true optimum — so the bound demonstrably does work.

## Reading the tables

- `p_s` is the fraction of 50 seeded runs reaching the exact optimum.
- **Approximation ratio** is `E_found / E_optimal`, kept in the brief's form for
  comparability with the literature. HP energies are negative or zero, so **1.0 is
  optimal and smaller is worse** — the reverse of the usual orientation. A *negative*
  ratio means the solver returned an infeasible state whose energy is positive.
- **TTS** is `t_run · ln(0.01) / ln(1 − p_s)`, the time to see the optimum at least once
  with 99% confidence. It reads "not reached" where `p_s = 0`, because no number of
  repetitions reaches that confidence and a large number there would look like a
  measurement.

## Honest limitations

1. **Sizes are small.** Exhaustive enumeration gives an exact optimum only to about
   N = 10, and every claim here sits inside that range. Nothing is demonstrated about
   scaling to peptides of biological interest.
2. **Energies are HP, not MJ.** Contact energies are 0 or −1. The Miyazawa–Jernigan
   matrix is declared as an optional extra but is not wired in, so the "realistic runs"
   the brief asks for have not been done.
3. **No quantum solver was benchmarked**, so the central comparison is incomplete.
4. **Simulated bifurcation is lightly tuned.** Its constants come from the literature,
   normalised for this problem's coupling spread. This is a statement about bSB *with
   literature defaults on a penalty-heavy QUBO*, not a proof that no bSB configuration
   can solve the problem. A dedicated tuning study might narrow the gap.
5. **The published Hamiltonian does not enforce global self-avoidance.** Its ground state
   was measured to be a valid fold at every size tested, but that is an empirical
   observation up to N = 9, not a proof. `enforce_global_saw=True` makes it a guarantee,
   at a locality that grows with N.
6. **Every solver is single-threaded Python.** Wall-clock and TTS compare the algorithms
   as implemented here, not the hardware they would run on. bSB's parallelism is real and
   measured — a batch of 64 replicas costs far less than 64 sequential runs — but its
   advantage on wide silicon is not something this benchmark can demonstrate.

## Reproducing

```bash
uv sync
make bench      # full sweep, 50 seeds; about 55 minutes, slow by design
make figures
foldq report --label full
```

CI runs `make bench-fast`, a reduced sweep on the same code path, so the pipeline cannot
silently rot between full runs.
